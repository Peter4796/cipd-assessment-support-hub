Needs more critical analysis is the most common comment on CIPD assignment feedback, and one of the least explained. Tutors write it in the margin, assessors cite it in referral reports, and learners are left to guess what to do differently. Understanding what the phrase asks for, and how to deliver it, is often the difference between a referral and a strong pass, especially from Level 5 onwards.

This guide is the hub of the critical analysis cluster and covers the skill from the ground up: what critical analysis is and is not, why CIPD assessment is built around it, the moves that make writing critical on the page, what changes at each level, how to question published sources and workplace practice without arrogance, and a step-by-step method for making any answer more critical, finishing with a worked example, the common myths and the costliest mistakes. Companion guides take individual elements deeper and are signposted where they help.

## What critical analysis is

Description tells the reader what something is. Analysis examines why it matters, how it works, and what its strengths and limitations are. Critical analysis goes further still: it questions assumptions, compares perspectives, weighs the quality of the evidence, and reaches a reasoned judgement that the reader can follow and could, in principle, challenge. That last clause matters. A piece of critical analysis puts something at stake. If nothing in your paragraph could be disagreed with, nothing in it has been argued.

In CIPD work, critical analysis usually looks like some combination of the following.

- Comparing two models or approaches rather than describing one
- Weighing the strengths and limitations of an idea instead of listing its features
- Questioning whether evidence is strong, relevant and current before relying on it
- Considering context, and whether something that works in general would work for this organisation
- Surfacing assumptions, both the author's and your own
- Reaching a justified conclusion that the evidence has earned

None of these moves requires unusual intelligence or years of academic training. They require a habit: treating every claim, including your own, as something to be tested rather than transmitted. Learners who think sharply at work often write descriptively simply because nobody has shown them what the habit looks like in prose. That is what the rest of this guide does.

## What critical analysis is not

Three misreadings cause most of the trouble. First, critical does not mean negative. You can analyse a model rigorously and conclude that it is useful, well evidenced and a good fit for the organisation in question. What earns the credit is the quality of the weighing, not the direction of the verdict. An answer that attacks everything is no more critical than an answer that praises everything; both have skipped the judgement and jumped to a posture.

Second, critical analysis is not summary, however accurate. Explaining a theory clearly tells the assessor you have understood it, not what you make of it, and what you make of it is the thing being assessed. Summary is the floor analysis stands on, not a substitute for it.

Third, critical analysis is not opinion. A position asserted without evidence is just assertion, and strong wording does not strengthen it. The critical part of critical analysis lives in the reasoning that connects evidence to conclusion: the reader should see exactly why you have landed where you have, and what would have made you land elsewhere.

## Why CIPD assessments demand it

The people profession has spent years moving towards evidence-based practice: the principle that decisions about people should rest on the best available evidence, critically appraised, rather than on habit, fashion or instinct. That commitment shapes assessment directly. A task asking you to evaluate approaches to reward is rehearsing exactly what the profession will ask of you when a director wants to know whether the bonus scheme is working: gather the evidence, weigh its quality, compare the options, and recommend with reasons you can defend. Critical analysis is not an academic hoop separate from practice. It is the written form of professional judgement.

There is a marking reason too. Assessors award depth against your brief's command verbs, and analyse and evaluate cannot be satisfied by recall. A beautifully organised catalogue of facts meets an explain task and fails an evaluate task, because the marks for evaluation sit in the reasoning, and the reasoning is the one thing a descriptive answer never shows. Feedback saying needs more critical analysis almost always means the treatment stopped one rung below where the verb asked you to climb.

## The moves of critical thinking on the page

Critical thinking sounds abstract until you break it into moves, and the moves are small, repeatable and learnable. Five of them do most of the work in CIPD writing.

- Questioning claims: when a source, a model or a manager asserts something, ask how we know it is true, and how we would know if it were not
- Weighing evidence quality: distinguish a peer-reviewed study from a consultancy survey from an anecdote, and lean on each only as hard as it can bear
- Comparing perspectives: set two readings of the same issue against each other and work out where and why they diverge, rather than presenting one as the whole story
- Surfacing assumptions: name what a claim takes for granted, such as a stable labour market, a co-located workforce or a particular management style, and ask whether it holds here
- Reaching justified judgement: land on a position, state it plainly, and show the weighing that earned it

Most strong paragraphs use two or three of these moves, and the last is non-negotiable: questioning, weighing and comparing without ever concluding reads as indecision, because the judgement is the product the other moves exist to manufacture.

## Description, analysis and evaluation: a ladder

It helps to picture the three registers as rungs on a ladder, because each one stands on the one below. Description establishes what is. Analysis takes it apart: why it works, what drives it, where it is strong and weak. Evaluation reassembles the parts into a judgement about worth, fit or priority. Here is the same subject on all three rungs, using a generic workplace practice.

Description: the organisation runs an exit interview with every leaver, using a standard question set. Analysis: exit interviews capture reasons for leaving, but their timing weakens them, because by the time someone is interviewed the decision is made, so the data arrives too late to retain that person and reflects only those willing to be candid on the way out. Evaluation: exit interviews are worth keeping as a low-cost data source, but they should not be the organisation's main retention tool; a structured stay conversation at six months addresses the same risk while there is still time to act, and deserves the greater investment.

Notice what changes at each rung: the first sentence reports, the second reasons about cause and limitation, and the third commits to a judgement with a consequence attached. The command verb in your brief tells you which rung each task lives on, so match the rung to the verb.

> Tutor tip: after every point you make, ask so what. If your answer moves to consequences, comparison or judgement, you are analysing. If it just adds more description, you are not there yet, and the question needs asking again.

## Where critical analysis sits at each level

At Level 3, the demand is light. Briefs mostly ask you to explain and describe, and marks flow from accuracy, clarity and sensible workplace examples. A touch of criticality still lifts an answer, such as noticing one limitation or one situation where an approach would not work, but sustained evaluation is not the expectation.

At Level 5, critical analysis becomes a core requirement rather than a bonus. Analyse and evaluate arrive in force, tasks expect application to a real organisational context, and too descriptive becomes the most common driver of referrals. A Level 5 answer that explains three models accurately but never weighs, compares or judges them has done Level 3 work against Level 5 criteria.

At Level 7, criticality runs through everything. Postgraduate work is argument-led: you are expected to take positions on contested questions, engage seriously with opposing readings, treat sources as voices to be weighed rather than authorities to be cited, and sustain a line of reasoning across a whole submission. For the full postgraduate treatment, Building Critical Arguments in 7CO01 covers argument construction in depth for that unit; the foundations in this guide are the same skill one level down.

These are general patterns across the qualification framework, not rules for your specific unit. Always check your own current brief and your study centre's guidance, because the document in front of you defines what your submission actually needs.

## How to be critical of published sources without arrogance

Learners often hesitate here: who am I to question a professor or a published study? But questioning a source is not claiming to be cleverer than its author. It is asking the questions any careful professional reader asks, and researchers expect their work to be read this way. Four questions cover most of the ground.

- Method: how was the claim established, on what kind of sample, with what measures, and would a different method have found something different
- Context: where and when was the work done, and does its setting resemble the one you are writing about closely enough for the findings to transfer
- Currency: has the world the research describes moved on, through technology, regulation or labour market change, in ways that date its conclusions
- Fit: even if the work is sound and current, does it actually apply to the organisation, sector or workforce your task is about

Saying, with reasons, that a finding may not travel across sectors, or that a study from before a major shift in working patterns describes a world that no longer exists, is not arrogance. It is exactly the appraisal that evidence-based practice requires, and the tone stays respectful because the challenge is aimed at applicability, not the author. The strongest versions of this move set sources against each other: letting one study complicate another does the critical work for you and shows genuine reading at the same time.

## Being critical of workplace practice

The same discipline applies to your own organisation, and CIPD tasks frequently ask for it. Workplace practices survive for many reasons, and effectiveness is only one of them: habit, precedent, a senior sponsor, or the simple fact that nobody has asked lately. The critical questions are why do we do it this way, what evidence supports it, what would tell us it is working, and what alternatives exist. An annual appraisal cycle, a nine-box talent grid or a fixed induction programme are not beyond question just because they are familiar. Treating your own organisation's practice as a claim to be tested, rather than a fact to be reported, instantly raises the register of an answer, and it is exactly the behaviour the profession asks of practitioners.

## A method for making any answer more critical

1. Reread the task and its command verb, and decide what final judgement the task is really asking you to reach.
2. Draft that judgement as a single sentence before you write the section, even if it changes later; a section with a destination cannot wander.
3. Gather evidence from more than one side: at least two sources or perspectives that do not simply agree.
4. For each piece of evidence, note one strength and one limitation of relying on it, using the method, context, currency and fit questions.
5. Write the section as claim, evidence, complication, response and judgement, rather than as topic, facts and summary.
6. Apply the context test: state whether your judgement would hold in the organisation or scenario the brief sets, and why.
7. End on the consequence: say what should follow from your judgement, for the organisation, the argument or the profession.
8. Reread the section asking what a well-informed colleague could push back on; if there is nothing to push against, there is no argument yet.

## Worked example: turning description into critical analysis

The example below is entirely fictional. Amara is an invented Level 5 learner, Fenwood Stores is a made-up retail chain, and every figure is constructed for illustration. Her task asks her to evaluate the organisation's approach to employee voice.

Amara's first draft reads like this: Fenwood Stores runs an annual engagement survey and quarterly team briefings. The survey is sent to all staff and response rates are around 60 per cent. Results are shared with managers, who are asked to create action plans. Team briefings cascade information from head office to stores. These are the main channels through which employees can have a voice at Fenwood. Every sentence is accurate, and the paragraph evaluates nothing: it is an inventory wearing the clothes of an answer.

Her rewrite: Fenwood's voice arrangements are better at sending messages down than carrying them up, which limits their value as voice rather than communication. The annual survey does gather employee views, but a 60 per cent response rate leaves the quietest four in ten unheard, and an annual cycle means a concern can wait most of a year before anyone sees it. More tellingly, both channels are controlled by the organisation: the survey asks only the questions head office chooses, and briefings cascade decisions already made. Neither gives employees a route to raise issues on their own terms, which is the tougher test of genuine voice. On balance, Fenwood has communication mechanisms wearing the label of voice, and its priority should be a channel employees can initiate, such as an elected forum with a standing slot for store-raised issues.

What changed is worth naming. The rewrite opens with a claim instead of a fact, weighs the evidence rather than reporting it, surfaces the hidden assumption that a survey equals voice, and closes with a judgement that carries a consequence. No new information was needed; the same material was made to argue.

## Common myths about critical analysis

Myth one: critical means negative. It does not, as covered above, but the myth persists because everyday English uses critical to mean fault-finding. In assessment it means evaluative: the verdict can be positive, negative or conditional, provided it is earned.

Myth two: published theory is beyond question. The opposite is true: the classics attract the most scrutiny. Maslow's hierarchy is among the most cited models in management education, and questioning how well its strict ordering of needs holds across different cultures, incomes and workplaces is standard scholarly practice, not disrespect. A model's fame tells you it is influential; only examination tells you whether it fits your case.

Myth three: being balanced means not concluding. Balance describes the weighing, not the verdict. You consider the strongest reading on each side, concede what each gets right, and then land. Presenting both sides and refusing to judge is not balance; it is leaving the task unfinished, and assessors mark it accordingly.

## Common mistakes

- Answering an evaluate task with an accurate explanation, one rung below the verb
- Quoting only sources that agree, leaving nothing to weigh
- Saving all evaluation for a final paragraph instead of arguing throughout
- Asserting judgements without showing the reasoning that earned them
- Hedging every claim into neutrality so no position is ever taken
- Criticising a model in general terms without connecting the criticism to the organisation or context in the task
- Adding the phrase critically analyse to a descriptive paragraph and changing nothing else

## FAQs

### Does critical analysis mean finding faults?

No. It means reaching a justified judgement after weighing evidence and perspectives, and that judgement can be favourable. Concluding that a model is robust and fits the organisation, with reasons, is critical analysis. Listing complaints without weighing anything is not.

### How do I add critical analysis without blowing the word count?

Convert rather than add. Most descriptive drafts contain more reporting than the task needs, so cut the inventory down to what the argument uses, and spend the recovered words on weighing and judgement. A critical paragraph is rarely longer than a descriptive one; it spends its words differently.

### How many sources do I need before I can be critical?

Fewer than most learners fear, but they must earn their place. Two sources that read the same issue differently generate more critical analysis than six that agree. Depth of engagement matters more than the length of the reference list, though your brief and centre guidance set any formal expectations.

### Is critical analysis different at Level 3, Level 5 and Level 7?

The skill is the same; the dose changes. Level 3 rewards clear explanation with occasional critical touches, Level 5 makes analysis and evaluation a core requirement applied to organisational context, and Level 7 expects sustained argument throughout. Check your current brief for what your unit actually demands.

3CO04, Essentials of people practice, hands you the widest brief in the Level 3 Foundation Certificate in People Practice. Recruitment, selection, onboarding, performance, reward and the end of employment can all appear in one assignment, and the real structural challenge is not writing brilliantly about any single topic. It is making sure everything gets covered, in a sensible order, with enough words left for the final tasks. That is a structure problem, and structure problems have practical solutions.

This article is about structure and nothing else. For the full picture of what 3CO04 covers and how it is assessed, start with the 3CO04 complete guide, which is the hub of this series. One caveat before anything else: briefs vary by study centre and change over time, so treat everything here, especially the illustrative skeleton, as a way of thinking rather than a description of your actual brief. Your own current brief and your centre's guidance always come first.

## Let the employee lifecycle be your spine

The employee lifecycle describes the stages a person moves through with an organisation: attraction and recruitment, selection, induction and onboarding, performance and development, reward and retention, and finally the end of employment. Almost every task a 3CO04 brief can set belongs to one of those stages, which makes the lifecycle a ready-made ordering principle for the whole assignment.

Use it literally. Where your brief allows, arrange your sections in lifecycle order, so the assignment reads as one person's journey through an organisation rather than a pile of unrelated topics. The order does two structural jobs at once. It makes gaps visible, because a missing stage is easy to spot in a sequence and easy to miss in a heap. And it saves words, because each section inherits context from the one before it instead of starting cold.

If your brief numbers its tasks in a different order, follow the brief, because its numbering always wins. You can still use the lifecycle privately as a checklist, placing each task against its stage, so nothing the brief asks for gets lost along the way.

## Coverage is a structure problem, so solve it structurally

The most common reason capable learners come unstuck in 3CO04 is not misunderstanding a concept; it is running out of room. Early answers swell, later answers starve, and the tasks at the end of the brief, often the ones about performance, reward or leaving, get answered in a hurry with whatever words remain.

The fix happens before drafting, not during it. Give every task its own bounded section with its own heading, so no answer can quietly absorb its neighbour. Then set a word budget for each section, in proportion to how many parts the task carries, and write the number next to the heading in your plan. Budgets protect the later tasks from the earlier ones: when a section reaches its number, you finish the point and move on, and the words for the final task are still there when you arrive.

If a section runs over, trim description and repetition inside that section rather than borrowing from the sections still to come. Definitions need stating once, and scene-setting rarely earns marks.

> If the breadth of 3CO04 feels overwhelming, take heart: no single answer needs to be deep. The unit asks for clear, complete essentials, and a learner with a section plan and a word budget is already most of the way there.

## The explain-then-example shape for Level 3 answers

At Level 3, the command verbs lean towards explain and describe, and those answers reward a simple three-move shape. First, say what the idea is in plain English, as if explaining it to a colleague who is new to people practice. Second, give one workplace example, drawn from your own job, a previous role or a clearly invented organisation. Third, say why it matters, to the organisation, to the person, or to both.

Idea, example, significance. The example is the move learners most often drop, especially in the later tasks as energy runs low, and its absence is easy for a marker to see. One specific, believable example explained properly is worth more than three mentioned in passing. Keeping the same example organisation running through as many answers as possible saves words too, because you introduce it once and reuse it everywhere.

## Keep recruitment and selection separate on the page

Recruitment is attracting a pool of suitable candidates; selection is choosing between the candidates you attracted. They are neighbouring stages, and briefs often ask about both, which is exactly why they blur together in drafts. Give each its own clearly labelled section or block of paragraphs, even when a single task covers both, and finish everything you want to say about attracting candidates before you start on choosing between them.

The page order carries meaning here. An answer that hops from job adverts to interview questions and back again reads as confusion, even when every individual sentence is accurate. Two clean blocks in lifecycle order read as understanding, and they make it easy for the marker to credit each stage separately.

## Practical elements: follow the brief's format exactly

Some 3CO04 briefs include practical elements alongside the written answers, such as drafting workplace materials or writing up a practical activity. The specifics vary by centre, so this can only be described generically, but the structural rule is constant: the format instructions in your brief are part of the task, not decoration around it.

Give each practical element its own section, produce it in exactly the shape the brief specifies, and resist folding it into an essay-style answer. If the brief asks for a document, produce something that looks like that document. If it asks for a write-up of an activity, cover what the brief says the write-up must include, point by point. Marks attach to the requested format, and a polished essay in the wrong shape loses them.

## An illustrative skeleton

Here is how the shape can come together for a generic 3CO04-style brief. This is an illustration only, not your brief: centres set different tasks with different formats and weightings, so map the ideas onto the tasks you have actually been given.

1. A short introduction naming your example organisation. Around 5 per cent of the words.
2. Recruitment: attracting candidates, in the explain-then-example shape. Around 15 per cent.
3. Selection: choosing between candidates, kept clearly separate. Around 15 per cent.
4. Induction and onboarding. Around 10 per cent.
5. Performance and development. Around 15 per cent.
6. Reward and retention. Around 15 per cent.
7. The end of the employment relationship. Around 15 per cent.
8. Any practical elements in the exact format the brief specifies, plus a brief conclusion, with the remaining words.

## A planning method for 3CO04

1. List every task and every part of every task from your brief on one page, so the full width of the unit is visible before you write anything.
2. Place each task against its lifecycle stage, and set your section order: the brief's order if it fixes one, lifecycle order if the choice is yours.
3. Set a word budget for every section, in proportion to how many parts it carries, and write the number beside each heading.
4. Choose one example organisation, real or clearly invented, and note the example you will use for every task before drafting any of them.
5. Draft each answer in the explain-then-example shape: the idea in plain English, one workplace example, why it matters.
6. Draft any practical elements directly from the brief's format instructions, ticking off each required feature as you include it.
7. When you review, check the later sections first, because that is where thinness hides, then check every task against the brief line by line.

## Common structural mistakes in 3CO04

- The tour-of-everything answer, which mentions every topic the learner revised instead of answering the specific task in front of them.
- Starved final sections, where the last tasks in the brief get a fraction of the room they need because earlier answers overran.
- Examples that disappear after the first task or two, leaving the later answers as accurate but ungrounded explanation.
- Recruitment and selection blurred into one account of hiring, which costs easy marks across two separate stages.
- Practical elements written as essays, ignoring the format the brief asked for.
- No visible section boundaries, so the marker has to hunt for where one task's answer ends and the next begins.

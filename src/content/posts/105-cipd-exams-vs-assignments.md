Ask a room of prospective CIPD learners what worries them most and someone always mentions exams: the silent hall, the memorised definitions, the three-hour scramble against the clock. Whether the qualification means CIPD exams or assignments is one of the most common questions people ask before enrolling, and plenty of new learners arrive still unsure what they have signed up for.

Here is the short answer. CIPD qualifications are assessed mainly through written assignments rather than sit-down exams. The exact assessment format for any unit is set by the unit and by your study centre, so always check your centre's information for the course you are considering or studying. But for most learners the experience is assignment-based from start to finish, and that changes almost everything about how you should study, especially if your habits were formed in an exam hall.

> Reassurance note: if exams were never your strength, this is genuinely good news. Assignment-based assessment rewards steady work, real workplace insight and clear writing, none of which depend on how well you perform in a silent room with a ticking clock.

## What assignment-based assessment means in practice

Three features define the format, and each one is the opposite of what an exam trains you for.

It is open-book. You keep access to your reading, your notes and your organisation's real practices throughout. Nobody is testing whether you can reproduce a model from memory; the question is whether you can use it, apply it to a scenario or to your own workplace, and show your reasoning.

It is time-managed over weeks, not hours. A deadline sits some weeks away, and how you spend the time in between is entirely up to you. The exam hall's fierce external structure is gone, and self-management takes its place as the skill that actually decides outcomes.

It is criteria-marked. Your work is assessed against the assessment criteria set out for the unit, and your brief tells you what the marker is looking for before you write a word. That is a gift no exam ever gave you, and learners from exam backgrounds routinely underuse it. Whatever else you do, read your current brief and your centre's guidance closely, because they define the target.

## Why this suits working professionals, and why it still feels hard

For working adults, the format is a quiet advantage. You can apply every unit to your own organisation, which makes the reading feel relevant and gives you examples no textbook could supply. There is no memorisation cliff in the final week, no revision timetable competing with your job, and the work slots around a working life in a way that exam preparation never could. Your experience is not a distraction from the assessment; it is raw material for it.

And yet most learners find it harder than they expected, for an honest reason: the difficulty has moved rather than disappeared. Sustained self-management replaces the crammed fortnight, and nobody is standing over you in week three. Writing skill matters far more, because structure, argument and referencing carry the marks that recall used to. And there is no adrenaline deadline until the real one, which is why so many capable people drift for five weeks and then meet the format's only genuinely dangerous feature: a final week that cannot absorb all the work.

## Studying for assignments is a different sport

If your study habits were built for exams, the habits themselves are not wrong, but several point in the wrong direction now.

Reading changes from recall to use. Exam revision rewards covering everything and remembering it. Assignment reading rewards selecting what serves the task in front of you and capturing it in a usable form, with the reference recorded on the spot. Reading everything before writing anything is an exam habit in disguise, and it quietly eats the weeks the draft needed.

Notes change from topic to task. Revision notes summarise subjects, because you cannot predict the exam question. But you have the assignment question already, so organise notes under the task they serve: everything for task one in one place, with page references and a thought about how you will use it. Notes organised by topic have to be searched all over again at drafting time; notes organised by task turn into the skeleton of the answer.

Practice changes from past papers to drafting. In exam study, practice means answering old questions under time pressure. Here, practice means drafting early and revising often, because your first version of a section is a draft you can improve, not a performance you must perfect. The learners who do best treat writing as part of studying, not as the event that happens after studying.

## Habits to keep, habits to drop

Some exam-honed habits transfer beautifully, so keep these.

- Planning and timetabling: the discipline of a revision timetable becomes the discipline of a backwards plan with milestones.
- Active reading: summarising in your own words and testing your understanding matter as much as ever; only the purpose shifts from recall to application.
- Timed sessions: the focus of a timed past-paper hour works just as well pointed at ninety minutes of reading or drafting.

Others need to be consciously dropped, because they actively hurt assignment work.

- Cramming: a final-week surge cannot rescue an assignment, because reading, drafting, revising and referencing cannot be compressed into days without the quality collapsing.
- Memorising definitions: markers can see the module handbook too; reciting it back demonstrates nothing. Marks live in application and analysis.
- Question-spotting: there is nothing to spot. The task is in front of you, and the winning move is deep engagement with the brief you have, not prediction.

## What this means for planning a unit

The practical consequence of all this is simple: little and often beats intensity. An assignment built across six steady weeks, in two or three protected sessions a week, will nearly always beat the same hours spent in one heroic fortnight, because the format rewards revision, reflection and distance from your own draft. That rhythm, and how to defend it around a full-time job, is the whole subject of the pillar guide Managing CIPD Deadlines While Working Full-Time: The Complete Guide.

## Adapting exam-honed habits: a method

1. Read your current brief and your centre's guidance before anything else, and write down in your own words what each task is asking and what the criteria reward.
2. Replace the revision timetable with a backwards plan: milestones for brief decoded, reading done, first draft, review and submission, each with a date.
3. Set up task-based notes from day one, one section per assignment task, and record every reference the moment you use a source.
4. Start drafting far earlier than feels comfortable, ideally as soon as the first task's reading is done, and treat the draft as something you will revise at least twice.
5. Keep your timed-session habit, but point it at reading and drafting blocks instead of practice questions.
6. Convert the week you would have spent cramming into a review week: check the draft against every criterion, tighten the writing and finish the referencing properly.
7. When instinct says memorise this, ask instead where in my organisation have I seen this, because application is what the format rewards.

## A worked example: Amina retools her study habits

Amina is a fictional learner, invented for this example. She is a finance-turned-HR administrator at Bryden and Cole, a made-up insurance firm, who passed a string of professional accounting exams through formidable discipline: colour-coded revision timetables, flashcards, past papers, and a legendary final-week cram. Starting her CIPD Level 3, she did what had always worked, spending the first month making flashcards of definitions and planning a revision surge for the fortnight before the deadline.

The brief unsettled her. It did not ask her to define anything from memory; it asked her to explain and apply ideas to a workplace, with examples. Her flashcards contained the raw material but nothing about her own organisation, and none of it was in writable form. Five weeks in, she had memorised a great deal and drafted nothing.

The fix was reallocation, not more effort. She rebuilt her notes under the assignment tasks, folding in examples from her own workplace beside each concept. She kept her beloved timed sessions but pointed them at drafting, one task section per Saturday morning. The final fortnight, which old habit had reserved for cramming, became a review fortnight: draft checked against the brief, writing tightened, references completed at leisure. The discipline that passed her accounting exams passed this too; it just needed re-aiming.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating deadline week like an exam cram, when drafting, revising and referencing cannot be compressed into days.
- Memorising model answers or definitions, which earns nothing without application to the task and your context.
- Underestimating referencing time, which is a real workload of its own and always takes longer than the last night allows.
- Reading everything before writing anything, an exam habit that quietly consumes the drafting weeks.
- Ignoring the assessment criteria, when they are the closest thing to a mark scheme you will ever be handed in advance.
- Assuming the format is easier because there is no exam, then discovering that self-management was the hard part all along.

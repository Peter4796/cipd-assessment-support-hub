Most 5CO02 briefs are really two assignments woven together. One strand asks discussion questions about evidence-based practice, critical thinking and creating value. The other hands you data, often a table of figures, and asks you to analyse it, present your findings and draw conclusions. The two strands need different shapes on the page, and a lot of structural problems in this unit come from writing every answer the same way.

This article is about structure and nothing else. If you want the full picture of what 5CO02 covers and how it is assessed, start with the 5CO02 complete guide, which is the hub of this series. One caveat before anything else: briefs vary by study centre and change over time, so treat everything here, especially the illustrative skeleton, as a way of thinking rather than a description of your actual brief. Your own current brief and your centre's guidance always come first.

## Start by decoding the brief into task types

Before you plan a single section, go through your brief and label every task as one of two types. Discussion tasks ask you to explain, discuss or evaluate ideas: what evidence-based practice means, how critical thinking improves decisions, how people practice creates value for stakeholders. Data tasks give you figures and ask you to do something with them: calculate, present, interpret and conclude.

The label matters because it tells you the shape of the answer. A discussion task is an argument that runs from evidence to a landing point. A data task is a demonstration that runs from method to implication. Mixing the shapes up produces the two classic 5CO02 failures: discussion answers that drift without ever concluding, and data answers that present numbers without saying what they mean.

Some tasks are hybrids, asking you to interpret given data and then discuss what the organisation should do about it. Treat those as a data answer followed by a short discussion answer, in that order, under one heading.

## The four-part shape for every data task

Data task answers reward a repeatable structure. Use the same four moves every time, in the same order.

1. Method. Say briefly what you did with the data: what you calculated, how, and why that measure suits the question. One or two sentences is usually enough, but they matter, because they show the marker your working.
2. Results. Present what you found in a labelled table or chart, with a short title, a number so you can refer to it, and units and time periods made clear. The visual carries the numbers so your text does not have to repeat them all.
3. Interpretation. Say what the results show: the pattern, the comparison, the trend, and anything surprising. This is where you move from numbers to meaning, and it is where most of the marks live.
4. Implication. Say what the finding means for the organisation and what should happen next. Even one firm sentence here turns analysis into evidence-based practice, which is the whole point of the unit.

The four parts do not need subheadings inside the answer; they are a rhythm, not a template. But if you check each data answer against the list and find one of the four missing, you have found a structural gap before the marker does.

> If a marker can see your table but has to hunt for what it means, the structure has failed. Keep each table and its interpretation together, in consecutive paragraphs, never separated by pages or pushed into an appendix.

## Structuring discussion answers around the evidence-to-recommendation line

Discussion tasks in 5CO02 are not invitations to share views. The unit is about evidence-based practice, and markers read your discussion answers looking for exactly that behaviour: claims backed by credible evidence, interpretation that is visible on the page, and a conclusion that follows from what came before.

A reliable shape for a discussion answer mirrors the data shape in four stages. Open by framing the question and defining any key terms in a sentence or two. Present the evidence: what credible sources, examples or organisational information say about the issue. Interpret it: what the evidence suggests, where sources disagree, and which reading you find most convincing and why. Then land: a clear concluding position or recommendation that a reader could act on.

The landing is the stage that matters most and gets skipped most. If a task asks you to discuss approaches to decision-making, a strong answer ends by saying which approach suits which situations and why, not by trailing off after describing the options.

## Where tables and charts sit, and how to refer to them

Tables and charts belong in the body of your answer, next to the text that discusses them, not gathered at the end of the document. Give each one a number and a short descriptive title, along the lines of Table 1, absence days per employee by department. Then refer to it by number in your text, so the reader is told that Table 1 shows absence concentrated in two departments rather than being left to notice it.

Never leave a visual to speak for itself. Every table or chart should be introduced before it appears and interpreted after it, so the reader never meets a block of numbers without knowing why it is there. Check your centre's rules on whether tables count towards the word limit, because the answer changes how much you should put in them.

Appendices, where your centre permits them, are for supporting material a curious reader might want, not for anything you expect to be marked. Analysis buried in an appendix is usually analysis the marker never credits.

## Word budgeting when marks and words pull apart

5CO02 has a structural quirk: data tasks often carry heavy marks but need surprisingly few words, because the table does so much of the work. A calculation and a labelled chart might absorb an hour of your effort and only a modest slice of your word budget. Discussion tasks are the opposite, consuming words quickly.

So budget in two passes. First allocate words roughly in proportion to the marks or criteria each task carries, then adjust for task type: trim the data tasks, because their visuals carry weight the word count does not measure, and give the recovered words to the discussion tasks and to the interpretation stage of each data answer. Interpretation is the one place a data task genuinely needs words, so protect it when you trim.

## An illustrative skeleton

Here is how the shape can come together for a generic 5CO02-style brief. This is an illustration only, not your brief: centres set different tasks with different weightings, so map the ideas onto the tasks you have actually been given.

1. Introduction setting scope and, if required, the organisational context. Around 5 per cent of the words.
2. Discussion section on evidence-based practice and decision-making, running from evidence through interpretation to a landed conclusion. Around 25 per cent.
3. Discussion section on creating value for the organisation and its people, in the same shape. Around 20 per cent.
4. First data task: method, labelled table or chart, interpretation, implication. Around 15 per cent.
5. Second data task, in the same four-part shape. Around 15 per cent.
6. A short section drawing the findings into overall recommendations, if your brief asks for them. Around 10 per cent.
7. A brief conclusion and a complete, consistent Harvard reference list with the remaining words.

## A planning method for 5CO02

1. Label every task in your brief as discussion, data or hybrid, and note the command verb in each.
2. Open the data before you plan anything else, and rough out the calculations, so you know what your tables will show.
3. Sketch each data answer as four bullet points: method, result, interpretation, implication.
4. Sketch each discussion answer as evidence, interpretation and the landing sentence you are working towards. Writing the landing sentence first keeps the whole answer aimed at it.
5. Budget words in proportion to marks, then shift words from data presentation to discussion and interpretation.
6. Draft the data tasks first, because their findings often feed the discussion answers and any final recommendations.
7. Check every table and chart is numbered, titled and referred to in the text, then review the whole draft against the criteria in your brief.

## Common structural mistakes in 5CO02

- Analysis buried in appendices, where markers rarely credit it. If it earns marks, it belongs in the body.
- Data separated from interpretation, so the tables sit in one place and the meaning in another, and the marker has to stitch them together.
- Discussion answers with no recommendation landing, which describe a debate but never conclude anything.
- Presenting results without showing the method, so the marker cannot tell what was calculated or why.
- One undifferentiated essay in which the data work is squeezed into prose instead of presented in labelled tables and charts.
- Word budgets that follow effort rather than marks, so hours of spreadsheet work crowd out the interpretation that actually scores.

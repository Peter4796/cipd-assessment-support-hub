Somewhere in almost every CIPD assignment, a theory arrives. Maslow's pyramid appears in an answer about engagement, Tuckman's stages in a question about teams, and the marker's interest fades a little, because the theory is usually recited rather than used. The learner proves they can remember it. What the assessment wanted was proof they can judge it.

This guide covers evaluating HR theories: how to treat a theory as a tool with strengths and limits rather than a fact to be reported, the questions that reveal whether a theory fits your task, and how to write theory paragraphs that earn analysis marks. It applies the wider skill set out in What Is Critical Analysis in CIPD? A Complete Guide to the specific case of theory, which is where most learners meet critical writing for the first time.

## A theory is a tool, not a truth

Every theory in the people profession is a simplification. A researcher looked at a messy slice of working life, noticed a pattern, and built a model that makes the pattern easier to see and talk about. That is what theories are for: they are lenses that bring some things into focus by pushing other things out of frame. A lens can be genuinely useful and still be the wrong lens for the scene in front of you.

This is why reciting a theory earns so little. Description proves you own the lens; analysis proves you know what it shows, what it hides, and whether it suits your situation. A theory earns its place in an answer the way a witness earns a place in a courtroom: by being relevant to this case and by standing up to cross-examination. Your job as the writer is to be the examiner, not the stenographer.

## Five questions to ask of any theory

Before a theory goes anywhere near your answer, put it through five questions. They work on any model, framework or typology the profession uses.

- What was it built to explain, and is that your situation? A model developed to explain one thing is often stretched to cover another, and the stretch marks show.
- What evidence supports it, and how strong is that evidence generally? Some theories rest on decades of replicated research; others became famous on thinner foundations and stayed famous through repetition.
- What has it been criticised for? Almost every well-known theory has a well-known critique, and markers know them; engaging with the critique is expected, not optional daring.
- What does it miss or simplify? Every lens crops the picture, and naming what sits outside the frame, whether context, culture, power or individual difference, is analysis in itself.
- Does it fit this organisation, this sector and this era? A model built around one kind of workplace in one period may translate poorly to a gig platform, a care home or a hybrid team today.

You will not have room to answer all five in every paragraph, and you do not need to. One or two of them, answered specifically for your scenario, are enough to turn a recital into an evaluation.

## Three familiar theories, treated critically

The pattern is easiest to see on theories everyone knows. The three below are handled at the level of common knowledge: widely taught ideas and widely repeated criticisms, with no claims about particular studies or figures. In your own work, any specific claim about what research found needs a credited source, as Using Evidence in CIPD Assignments: From Claim to Credit explains in full.

### Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Maslow's hierarchy may be the most recited model in people management, and a critical paragraph treats it with respect and suspicion at once. In assignment prose, that can read like this. Maslow's hierarchy offers a useful reminder that recognition schemes cannot compensate for pay people experience as insecure, since the model places security before esteem. Its widely noted weaknesses matter here too: the strict ordering of needs has long been criticised as too rigid, and the model carries assumptions about individual ambition that do not travel across all cultures or all workforces. For this organisation, the broad insight survives even if the strict sequence does not, so the analysis that follows leans on the distinction between security and recognition rather than on the pyramid itself.

### Herzberg's two-factor theory

Herzberg's split between hygiene factors and motivators remains genuinely useful, and it is also a theory whose research approach has been contested for decades, because the way people were asked about good and bad experiences at work plausibly shaped the pattern it reports. Critical prose wears both facts openly. Herzberg's two-factor theory suggests that fixing the rota chaos and the broken booking system will quiet dissatisfaction without creating engagement, which matches what the scenario describes. The theory's contested methodology is a reason for caution rather than rejection: the hygiene and motivator split is treated here as a prompt for investigation, not a law, so the recommendation addresses the irritants and invests in recognition and development, rather than assuming either alone will do.

### Tuckman's stages of group development

Tuckman's forming, storming, norming and performing sequence is memorable, which is both its strength and its danger. Critical prose can hold both. Tuckman's stages give the project team a shared language for the friction it is experiencing, and reframing early conflict as storming rather than failure has practical value for the team leader. The model's neatness is also its main limitation, as is widely observed: real teams loop back, skip stages and start again whenever membership changes, so the stages are better read as recurring modes than as a one-way journey. The recommendation therefore uses the model to normalise conflict during the restructure while avoiding any promise that the team will reach performing and stay there.

## Criticism without dismissal

Notice what those three paragraphs never do: they never dismiss. A criticised theory can still earn its place in your answer, provided you show that you know its limits and that your use of it survives them. Maslow's ordering may be shaky while the distinction between security and esteem stays illuminating; Herzberg's method may be contested while the hygiene warning stays practical; Tuckman may be too tidy while still giving a team words for its friction. This is the move markers reward, using a theory with your eyes open, because it demonstrates judgement rather than allegiance.

The reverse failure is just as common: savaging a theory to appear critical, then quietly relying on it anyway. If one paragraph demolishes Maslow and the recommendation two pages later rests on meeting basic needs first, the contradiction is visible from the marker's chair. Either defend the use, or change the recommendation.

## Pairing theories to expose what each misses

One of the fastest routes to genuine analysis is to put two theories in the same paragraph and let each show what the other leaves out. A theory of what people want says little about how they decide whether effort is worthwhile, and the reverse is also true; run one against the other and gaps open on both sides. The same works across topics: a stage model of change highlights sequence and misses politics, while a power-based reading highlights politics and misses sequence. You do not need many theories for this. You need two, chosen because they disagree, and one sentence of your own saying what the disagreement reveals about your case.

## Theory at each level

Expectations rise with level, in broad terms. At Level 3, naming a relevant theory accurately and applying it simply to a workplace example is usually the goal, with criticism welcome but light. At Level 5, markers expect application plus evaluation: the theory used on the scenario, at least one limitation acknowledged, and a view on how far it helps. At Level 7, critique is the point: theories weighed against evidence and against each other, with a defended position on which reading fits best. These are general patterns rather than rules, so always check your current assessment brief and your centre's guidance for what your unit actually asks.

## How to write a critical theory paragraph

1. Name the theory in one sentence, in your own words, at the level of detail the point needs and no more.
2. Apply it: say what the theory predicts or reveals about the specific situation in your answer.
3. Test it: raise the most relevant limitation, criticism or missing factor, chosen because it bears on this case rather than because it is famous.
4. Judge it: state how far the theory helps here, given that limitation.
5. Use it: let that judgement shape the recommendation or conclusion the paragraph feeds, so the theory visibly does work.

> Tutor tip: after drafting, find every sentence that describes a theory and ask what the next sentence does with it. If the next sentence moves to a new topic, the theory was decoration; give it a job or cut it.

## Common pitfalls

- Theory-dumping: summarising three models in a row with no application to the scenario between them
- Treating age as refutation, as though a theory from decades ago must be wrong because it is old; theories fail for reasons, and the reason is the analysis
- Criticising without offering an alternative reading, which leaves the marker a demolished theory and no way forward
- Savaging a theory and then building the answer on it anyway, unexamined
- Stretching a theory to a situation it was never built to explain without acknowledging the stretch
- Presenting a theory's most famous criticism as though you discovered it, instead of engaging with what it means for your use

Most 5HR03 briefs mix two kinds of task without announcing the change. One strand asks you to explain reward concepts: what reward strategy is, how financial and non-financial reward differ, what the total reward package means. The other strand asks you to evaluate: compare approaches to pay and benefits, weigh them against a specific organisation's circumstances, and justify a choice. The two strands need different shapes on the page, and most structural trouble in this unit comes from writing evaluative answers as if they were explanations with a longer word count.

This article is about structure and nothing else. If you want the full picture of what 5HR03 covers and how it is assessed, start with the 5HR03 complete guide, which is the hub of this series. One caveat before anything else: briefs vary by study centre and change over time, so treat everything here, especially the illustrative skeleton, as a way of thinking rather than a description of your actual brief. Your own current brief and your centre's guidance always come first.

## Start by decoding the brief into task types

Before you plan a single section, go through your brief and label every task as explanatory or evaluative. Explanatory tasks ask what something is or how it works: explain the principles of reward, describe the types of financial and non-financial reward, outline the factors that shape pay and benefits decisions. Evaluative tasks ask you to choose: compare approaches, assess suitability for a given context, justify a recommendation.

The label tells you the shape of the answer. An explanatory answer is a clear account that moves from definition to significance, and it can be relatively compact. An evaluative answer is an argument, and arguments have anatomy: they need options, criteria and a judgement, which takes more room. If you budget words evenly without noticing which type each task is, the evaluative answers end up too thin to hold a judgement, and that is exactly where 5HR03 marks concentrate.

Some tasks are hybrids, asking you to explain an approach and then assess whether it would suit the organisation in the brief. Treat those as a short explanatory answer followed by an evaluative one, in that order, under a single heading.

## The compare-and-justify spine

Evaluative answers in 5HR03 reward a repeatable structure. Use the same five moves every time, in the same order.

1. Context. Restate, in a sentence or two, the features of the organisation or scenario that will drive the decision: sector, size, goals and the pressures the brief mentions. This anchors everything that follows.
2. Options. Set out the approaches you are comparing, fairly and briefly. An option you plan to reject still deserves an honest account of what it offers.
3. Criteria. Make your comparison criteria explicit: cost and affordability, fit with strategy and culture, likely effect on motivation and retention, fairness. Named criteria turn a preference into an analysis.
4. Judgement. Compare the options against the criteria and say which you recommend, in plain terms, with the reasons visible on the page.
5. Limits. Acknowledge what your recommendation costs, risks or leaves unresolved, and say how the organisation would know it was working.

The five moves do not need subheadings inside the answer; they are a rhythm, not a template. But if you check an evaluative answer against the list and find no criteria, or no landed judgement, you have found the structural gap before the marker does.

> A useful test for any evaluative answer: could a reader who disagrees with your recommendation still see exactly why you made it? If the reasoning is visible enough to argue with, the structure is doing its job.

## Keep one organisational context running through the whole assignment

5HR03 justifications live or die on context, and context works best when it is consistent. Whether your brief supplies a scenario or lets you draw on your own organisation, decide on the context early, describe it once near the start, and then hold it steady. Every evaluative answer should return to the same organisation, the same pressures and the same goals.

The alternative, drifting between a supplied scenario, your employer and organisations in general, is a structural fault as much as a content one. It forces every answer to rebuild context from scratch, wastes words, and leaves the marker unsure which organisation your recommendation is actually for. One paragraph of well-chosen context near the top, referred back to throughout, is one of the cheapest structural wins in this unit.

## Where motivation theory sits in the structure

Motivation theory belongs inside your evaluative answers, at the moment of comparison, not in a standalone section that summarises theories and moves on. A sentence applying a well-known idea, such as the distinction between the factors that prevent dissatisfaction and the factors that actively motivate, earns its place when it explains why one reward option would work better than another for the people in your context.

Structurally, that means theory appears in short, applied touches at the judgement stage of the spine, never as a block. If you find you have written a paragraph that explains a theory without mentioning your organisation or the choice in front of it, move the useful insight into the relevant comparison and cut the rest. Summary paragraphs of theory are the most commonly cut material in strong 5HR03 drafts, and nobody misses them.

## Handle pay data carefully

Some 5HR03 briefs include pay material: salary ranges in a scenario, benchmarking information, benefit costs. The structural rule is simple: interpret what the brief supplies, and never invent figures to fill gaps. If your brief gives you numbers, refer to them precisely and build your comparisons around them. If it does not, argue from principles such as affordability, market position and fairness, and keep invented statistics off the page.

This matters because unsupported figures damage credibility in exactly the unit that is about justified decisions. A sentence saying that a targeted adjustment would close the gap for hard-to-fill roles is an argument; the same sentence with a precise invented percentage attached is a liability. Where you need external evidence about pay trends, use credible published sources and cite them; where you have none, state the principle instead.

## An illustrative skeleton

Here is how the shape can come together for a generic 5HR03-style brief. This is an illustration only, not your brief: centres set different tasks with different weightings, so map the ideas onto the tasks you have actually been given.

1. Introduction naming the organisational context and scope. Around 5 per cent of the words.
2. Explanatory section on reward principles and strategy, moving from definitions to why they matter for your context. Around 15 per cent.
3. Explanatory section on financial and non-financial reward and the total reward idea. Around 15 per cent.
4. Evaluative section comparing approaches to pay and benefits for the context, running the full compare-and-justify spine. Around 25 per cent.
5. Evaluative section on linking reward to performance, again through the spine, with motivation theory applied at the judgement stage. Around 20 per cent.
6. A short section drawing the recommendations together, with their limits and success measures. Around 10 per cent.
7. A brief conclusion and a complete, consistent Harvard reference list with the remaining words.

## A planning method for 5HR03

1. Label every task in your brief as explanatory, evaluative or hybrid, and note the command verb in each.
2. Fix your organisational context before you plan anything else, and write the single paragraph that describes it.
3. Sketch each evaluative answer as five bullet points: context, options, criteria, judgement, limits.
4. Write the judgement sentence for each evaluative task first, so the whole answer is aimed at a destination.
5. Choose one or two motivation theories and mark exactly where each will appear inside a comparison.
6. Budget words in proportion to marks, then shift words from explanation to evaluation, because judgement needs room.
7. Check any figures you have used against the brief or a credible source, and replace unsupported numbers with principles.
8. Review the whole draft against the criteria in your brief, task by task.

## Common structural mistakes in 5HR03

- Scheme-by-scheme description with no landed judgement, so the marker sees knowledge of reward practices but never a decision.
- Theory sections detached from the choices, summarising motivation theories in a block that connects to nothing.
- Moralising conclusions that skip the comparison, declaring that fair pay matters without ever weighing the options the task set.
- Context stated once and never used again, so recommendations read as if written for organisations in general.
- Invented pay figures used to make comparisons look precise, which weakens credibility instead of strengthening it.
- Word budgets that treat every task equally, starving the evaluative answers where the marks concentrate.

There is a moment in many CIPD assignments where the writer glimpses the opposing view and looks away. Acknowledging it feels dangerous, as though admitting that anyone disagrees will weaken the answer. The instinct is exactly backwards. An unopposed claim is not a strong claim; it is an assertion, and markers credit assertions lightly no matter how confidently they are written.

This guide covers counterarguments in CIPD assignments: why engaging with opposition raises marks, where to find a counterargument when nobody hands you one, the concede-and-respond pattern that handles opposition without surrendering to it, and a worked example showing a one-sided passage repaired. It applies the wider skill set out in What Is Critical Analysis in CIPD? A Complete Guide to the single most mark-rich move in critical writing.

## Why counterarguments raise marks

Evaluation, the behaviour markers reward most, literally means weighing, and weighing requires at least two things on the scales. When you present only the case for your view, no weighing has happened on the page, whatever happened in your head, and the marker can only credit what appears on the page. When you present the best case against your view and show why your position survives it, the marker watches the weighing happen. The same claim, held after visible testing, is worth more than the identical claim merely announced.

There is a credibility effect too. A writer who mentions no objections looks either unaware of them or afraid of them, and neither builds trust. A writer who states the objection fairly before answering it signals command of the whole debate, and markers, like all readers, extend more trust to positions that have visibly survived contact with their opposition.

## Where to find the counterargument

A counterargument rarely announces itself. Four places reliably hold one.

- The other side of a debate: most topics in people practice sit inside live disagreements, and whichever side your claim takes, the opposite side is your counterargument ready-made
- A criticised limitation: if your claim leans on a theory or a study, the known weaknesses of that theory or study point straight at the counterview
- A context where the claim fails: ask where your recommendation would not work, in a different sector, size, culture or budget, and the failure case is your counterargument
- A stakeholder who loses: every recommendation costs someone something, and the perspective of the person or group paying that cost is an opposing view worth stating

If all four searches come back empty, be suspicious. A claim with no conceivable opposition is usually too vague to oppose, which also makes it too vague to credit.

## The concede-and-respond pattern

The pattern has three moves. First, state the strongest opposing view fairly, in words its supporters would accept. Second, concede what it gets right, explicitly: name the point on which the opposition is correct. Third, respond: show why your position survives anyway, or, just as respectably, qualify your position to absorb what the concession taught you. A claim that emerges narrower and better defended has gained value, not lost it.

The order matters. Concession before response reads as strength: you gave the opposition its due and prevailed anyway. Response before concession reads as grudging, and a concession with no response at all reads as a change of mind, leaving the marker unsure what you actually think.

> Tutor tip: go through your draft and highlight every sentence that disagrees with your own position. No highlights means no evaluation on the page. One fairly stated counterview per major claim, each followed by a response, is the shape markers are looking for.

## Phrases that introduce opposition honestly

Small phrases do the signposting. However and on the other hand mark a turn. Critics argue and an alternative reading is introduce the opposing voice without endorsing it. This assumes is quietly powerful, because it points at the load-bearing premise of any claim, including your own. It could be objected that sets up a counterview you intend to answer. What matters is honesty: each phrase promises the reader a genuine opposing view, so the sentence that follows must contain one. A however followed by a restatement of your own position is a signpost to nowhere.

## The strawman trap

A strawman is a weakened version of the opposing view, built to be knocked down, and beating a weak version convinces nobody. Markers know the strong version of every standard debate, so a flattened opponent reads as evasion rather than victory. The test is whether someone who genuinely holds the opposing view would recognise your summary and accept it as fair; if they would wince, strengthen it. Counterintuitively, the stronger you make the opposing case, the more your answer earns for surviving it, so generosity towards your opponents is pure self-interest.

## Counterarguments at each level

In general terms, the expectation climbs with level. At Level 3, counterarguments are optional polish: a brief acknowledgement that another view exists can lift an answer, but the priority is accurate, applied understanding. At Level 5, they are expected wherever a task asks you to evaluate, because evaluation without opposition is description with a verdict attached. At Level 7, they are compulsory throughout, with every substantial claim tested against the strongest available opposition; Building Critical Arguments in 7CO01 covers that postgraduate treatment in depth. These are broad patterns rather than rules, so check your current assessment brief and your centre's guidance for what your unit requires.

## Keeping your own position

Concession is not surrender. The purpose of engaging opposition is to end up somewhere, and the somewhere must be yours: a judgement, stated and owned. Two failure modes lose it. The first concedes everything, agreeing with each view in turn until the conclusion has nothing left to conclude. The second balances forever, presenting both sides so even-handedly that the answer never lands. Weighing is the method; a judgement is the product. It is entirely respectable for that judgement to be conditional, holding for this context under stated conditions, but it must be a judgement.

## How to add a counterargument to an existing answer

1. Find your biggest claim: the sentence your answer most depends on, usually sitting near a recommendation or conclusion.
2. Write the strongest opposing view in one fair sentence, as its best advocate would put it.
3. Concede in one sentence what that view gets right about your case.
4. Respond in one or two sentences: show why your claim survives, or qualify the claim to absorb the concession.
5. Place the whole exchange before your conclusion, never after it, and check the conclusion still states a position.
6. Reread the opposing sentence on its own and ask whether a believer would accept it; if not, strengthen it and respond again.

## Worked example: flexible working, one-sided and repaired

The passage below is entirely fictional, and Ferndale Insurance is invented for illustration. One-sided, it reads like this. Ferndale Insurance should adopt fully flexible working across all departments. Flexibility improves wellbeing, saves office costs and widens recruitment, and staff surveys show that people want it. Rolling it out everywhere as soon as possible is clearly the right decision.

Repaired, with a fair counterview woven in and answered, it reads like this. Ferndale Insurance should extend flexible working, though not uniformly. The case is strong: flexibility can help wellbeing and recruitment, and the internal survey suggests real demand. The opposing case deserves weight, however. Critics of blanket flexibility would point out that Ferndale's claims teams depend on rapid informal collaboration and that new starters learn the work by absorption, both of which suffer when colleagues are remote on different days, and on this they are right. Yet that objection argues against uniform rollout, not against flexibility itself. A department-led model, with each team setting anchor days around its collaboration and onboarding needs, keeps the benefits the survey points to while answering the strongest objection to them, and the arrangement should be reviewed after six months.

Notice what changed. The claim narrowed and improved. The opposition got its best sentence, was conceded a genuine point, and was answered rather than dismissed. And the writer still holds a position at the end, which is the whole aim.

## Common pitfalls

- Token counterviews, raised and dismissed inside a single clause by a however that answers nothing
- Strawmen, opposing views summarised so weakly that their supporters would not recognise them
- Counterviews that arrive after the conclusion, where they cannot be answered and read as late-breaking doubt
- Conceding everything and concluding nothing, so the answer ends in a shrug
- Signpost phrases with no genuine opposition behind them, decoration pretending to be debate
- Inventing an opposing study or statistic just to knock it down; opposition must be as honest as evidence

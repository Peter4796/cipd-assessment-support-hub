5HR02 briefs cover a lot of ground: workforce planning and labour markets, resourcing and recruitment, talent and succession, turnover and retention. The structural risk is writing one long tour of everything, where topics blur into each other and the marker cannot see where any single criterion is met. The fix is a structure that gives each topic a clearly bounded section, while letting the whole submission read as one argument about one organisation.

This article is about structure and nothing else. For the full picture of the unit, start with the 5HR02 complete guide, which is the hub of this series, and for the deepest single topic, the workforce planning guide walks through the planning cycle in detail. One caveat before anything else: briefs vary by study centre and change over time, so treat everything here, especially the illustrative skeleton, as a way of thinking rather than a description of your actual brief. Your own current brief and your centre's guidance always come first.

## Give each topic a bounded section

Go through your brief and group the tasks under the unit's four broad areas: planning and labour markets, resourcing and recruitment, talent and succession, turnover and retention. Then give each area its own section with a heading that echoes the wording of the task it answers, so the marker can navigate straight to each criterion.

Bounded means each section opens by naming its question and closes by answering it, and material stays where it belongs. Retention arguments live in the retention section, not scattered through the other three. When a point genuinely spans two sections, make it once in full and refer back to it briefly, rather than repeating it. Boundaries are what stop a wide brief becoming a long blur.

## Introduce the organisation once

5HR02 rewards application, so almost every section needs an organisational context, whether that is your employer, a case study your centre supplies, or a clearly fictional organisation. Choose it before you plan, and introduce it exactly once, in your introduction: what it does, roughly how big it is, what its market and labour market look like, and the people pressures it currently faces. A short paragraph is enough.

After that, every section refers back to the same context instead of re-describing it. Re-introducing the organisation in each section wastes words you will want later, and it signals sections written in isolation. One introduction, then consistent reference, keeps the submission reading as a single piece of analysis about a single organisation.

## Explanation then application, in every section

Each section of a 5HR02 answer works best in two movements. First the concept, briefly: what workforce planning involves, what a recruitment method is good for, how a talent or succession approach works, with a credible source or two. Then the application, at length: what this means for your organisation, with named roles, its labour market conditions, and the data or examples that make the point specific.

Get the balance right. Level 5 marks sit mainly in application, so the explanation should be the shorter movement in every section, a platform rather than the performance. If a section reads like a textbook summary with a thin applied paragraph at the end, the proportions are inverted, and rebalancing them is usually the single biggest structural improvement available.

## Structuring answers built on turnover and retention data

When a task involves turnover or retention figures, whether supplied in your brief or described from your context, structure the answer as three moves: pattern, interpretation, action.

1. Pattern. Set out what the figures show, plainly: the headline rate, how it has moved over time, and where it concentrates by team, role or length of service. If your brief gives a table, present the key comparisons rather than repeating every number.
2. Interpretation. Ask what plausibly explains the pattern, consider more than one explanation, and say which the evidence favours and why. Turnover concentrated among new starters points somewhere different from an even spread, and the interpretation should show you know that.
3. Action. Recommend what the organisation should do, following directly from the interpretation, with some way of telling later whether it worked.

Keep the data physically close to the decisions it supports. Figures presented in one section while the actions they justify sit pages away force the marker to reassemble your argument, and some will not.

## Connect the sections into one workforce story

The four topics of 5HR02 are chapters of one story: the plan identifies the people the strategy needs and the gaps against that need, resourcing explains how to fill those gaps given the labour market, talent and succession grow the internal supply, and retention protects the whole investment. Strong submissions make that chain visible in the structure.

The tool for this is the linking sentence. End each section by handing off to the next, and open each section by picking up what the last one established: the gaps the plan found become the targets of the resourcing section, and the roles the talent section flagged as critical become the focus of succession and retention. A few sentences of connective tissue turn four competent essays into one argument, and the argument is what earns the higher bands.

> A quick coherence test: read only your section openings and closings. If they chain together into one story about the same organisation, the structure is working. If each could open a different assignment, it is not.

## An illustrative skeleton

Here is how the shape can come together for a generic 5HR02-style brief. This is an illustration only, not your brief: centres set different tasks with different weightings, so map the ideas onto the tasks you have actually been given.

1. Introduction setting scope, plus the single short introduction of your organisational context. Around 5 per cent of the words.
2. Workforce planning and labour markets, explanation then application. Around 25 per cent.
3. Resourcing, recruitment and selection, explanation then application, targeting the gaps the plan identified. Around 20 per cent.
4. Talent management and succession, explanation then application, flagging the critical roles. Around 20 per cent.
5. Turnover and retention, structured as pattern, interpretation, action. Around 20 per cent.
6. A conclusion that lands the workforce story, plus recommendations if your brief asks for them, and a complete Harvard reference list with the remaining words.

## A planning method for 5HR02

1. List every task in your brief, group the tasks under the four topic areas, and note the command verb in each.
2. Choose your organisational context and write its introduction paragraph first, so every section has the same anchor.
3. For each section, write one sentence of concept and one sentence of application before drafting, to fix the explanation-then-application balance early.
4. Sketch the story spine in four lines: what the plan finds, what resourcing must deliver, which roles need succession cover, and where retention is leaking.
5. Place your data: decide which section each figure or pattern supports, and plan it into that section next to the decision it justifies.
6. Budget words in proportion to the marks or criteria each task carries, keeping application ahead of explanation in every section.
7. Draft in story order, adding a linking sentence at each section boundary.
8. Review the draft against the criteria in your brief, checking that each section opens with its question and closes with its answer.

## Common structural mistakes in 5HR02

- Re-introducing the organisation in every section, which wastes words and betrays sections written in isolation.
- Topics answered in isolation, so the resourcing answer ignores the plan and the retention answer ignores both.
- Data quoted far from the decisions it supports, leaving the figures in one place and the actions they justify in another.
- Explanation crowding out application, so sections read as textbook summaries with a token applied paragraph.
- One long tour with few headings, where the marker has to excavate the criteria instead of being shown them.
- A conclusion that merely lists the topics covered instead of landing the workforce story with clear recommendations.

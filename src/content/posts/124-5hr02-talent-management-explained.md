## What talent management covers

Talent management is the deliberate work of identifying, developing, deploying and retaining the people who matter most to an organisation's performance. Each verb matters: identifying means deciding who counts as talent and how you would know, developing means investing in growth on purpose, deploying means putting people where they create most value, and retaining means keeping them long enough for the investment to pay back.

Organisations invest because capability is slow to copy: prices can be matched overnight, but a well led, well developed workforce takes competitors years to replicate. In assignment terms, that is your anchor: every talent activity should connect, visibly, to what the organisation is trying to achieve.

## Exclusive or inclusive: decide what talent means

The literature splits into two broad camps. The exclusive view defines talent as a select group: high-potential individuals, critical-role holders, the people whose loss would genuinely hurt. The inclusive view holds that everyone has talent, and that the organisation's job is to recognise and grow it across the whole workforce.

Each definition commits an organisation to different practice. An exclusive approach concentrates development budgets, accelerates a small group and makes succession pools easier to build, but risks misjudging potential, demotivating the unchosen majority and creating a self-fulfilling elite. An inclusive approach spreads development widely, lifts engagement and fairness, and surfaces unexpected capability, but stretches investment thinly and can leave critical roles without a focused pipeline.

The assignment move is straightforward: state which meaning you are using, justify it for your context, and apply it consistently. Many real organisations blend the two, an inclusive baseline with focused investment in critical roles, and naming the blend explicitly is itself a mark of understanding.

## The talent pipeline in practical terms

Talent management works as a pipeline of connected stages, and weak answers treat them as separate topics rather than a flow.

Attraction is the front end: the organisation's reputation, employer brand and overall offer determine who applies in the first place. Identification is where a definition of talent becomes a practice, as performance evidence, potential judgements and structured conversations decide who receives what investment. Development turns identification into growth through stretch assignments, coaching, mentoring, formal programmes and planned moves. Succession points development at continuity, preparing people for the roles the organisation cannot afford to leave empty. Retention protects the whole investment, because a pipeline that leaks at the end wastes everything upstream.

The stages compound: an organisation that attracts well but develops badly trains its competitors' future hires, and one that ignores retention pays for capability it never uses. When your brief asks about a single stage, a sentence locating it in the wider pipeline shows the joined-up understanding markers reward.

## The nine-box grid, used with care

The identification tool your assignment is most likely to involve is the nine-box grid, which plots people against current performance and estimated potential, producing nine cells. Its popularity is easy to explain: it is simple to grasp, it forces a conversation about potential rather than performance alone, and it gives managers a shared language for development decisions.

Its limits are just as real. Performance can be evidenced, but potential is a judgement, and judgements are vulnerable to bias: the halo effect from one good year, the visibility advantage of people who resemble their assessors, and the quiet penalty paid by capable people in unglamorous roles. Ratings also drift between raters, so one manager's rising star is another's steady performer.

Used with care, the grid is a prompt for evidence-based conversations, not a verdict: calibration sessions where managers challenge each other's placements, written and shared criteria for potential, and placements treated as provisional. At Level 5, knowing both why the grid is popular and where it misleads is exactly the explain-then-evaluate register the unit rewards.

## Succession planning basics

Succession planning applies talent thinking to continuity, and the operational logic runs in four moves. First, identify the critical roles: not necessarily the most senior, but the ones where an unplanned exit would do real damage. Second, assess readiness: who could step in now, who could be ready with development, and where the honest answer is nobody. Third, build development plans that close those readiness gaps, using stretch and exposure rather than courses alone. Fourth, review on a cycle, because people leave, plans age and the list of critical roles changes.

In an assignment, succession links talent management to business risk in one step: an empty critical role has a visible cost, and a credible internal pipeline is the cheapest insurance against it.

## Retention and the employee value proposition

Retention is where talent management is won or lost, and the employee value proposition is the main lever. The proposition is the whole deal a person gets for working somewhere: pay and benefits, development and progression, flexibility, the quality of management, and the meaning of the work itself. Talented people usually have options, and they stay when the whole deal beats the alternatives.

The useful insight is that the proposition can be tuned to the people the organisation most needs to keep. If skilled engineers are leaving for development opportunities elsewhere, the fix is progression and technical challenge, not a general pay review. Tie retention actions to what your identified talent actually values and your recommendations gain specificity.

## How to answer a talent task, step by step

Most talent tasks reward the same three moves: explain the concept accurately, apply it to a specific organisation with named roles, and evaluate whether the chosen approach fits the context. Check your own current brief and your centre's guidance for exact wording, then work through a method like this.

1. Define talent for your chosen context, stating whether you are using an exclusive, inclusive or blended meaning and why.
2. Fix the organisation, real or clearly fictional, and name the roles and skills that matter most to its performance.
3. Walk the relevant pipeline stages, saying what the organisation would actually do at each one rather than what the stage means.
4. Bring in one identification tool, such as the nine-box grid, and evaluate it honestly: what it adds, where it misleads, and how its limits would be managed.
5. Cover succession for the critical roles you named, with readiness assessments and development plans rather than a list of names.
6. Tie retention to the employee value proposition, matching the action to what the people you most need to keep actually value.
7. Close by evaluating the overall approach: why it fits this organisation, what its main risk is, and how its success would be measured.

> Tutor tip: if your talent answer never names a role, a pipeline stage or a specific development action, it is describing talent management rather than applying it, and description alone will not carry an application task.

## A worked example: Bramleigh Analytics

Bramleigh Analytics is a fictional data consultancy of two hundred people, invented for this example. Its growth depends on a small number of senior consultants who win work and lead client teams, and exit interviews suggest ambitious analysts leave when they cannot see a route to those roles.

Bramleigh chooses a blended definition and says so: an inclusive development baseline, because analytical capability across the firm is the product it sells, plus focused succession work on the senior consultant role, where the business risk concentrates. Identification uses nine-box conversations, with written criteria for potential and calibration between practice leads to blunt the subjectivity problem. Development for the succession pool is built from stretch, shadowing bids and leading small engagements, rather than courses. Retention work tunes the proposition for analysts, with a published progression route to senior consultant and transparent criteria.

The evaluation follows because every choice was justified along the way: the blend fits a firm whose whole workforce is its product but whose risk sits in one role, the main risk is that a visible succession pool demotivates those outside it, and success would be measured through senior consultant readiness and analyst retention. That is the explain, apply, evaluate shape in miniature.

## Common pitfalls

- Defining talent vaguely or not at all, so the answer drifts between meanings without noticing
- Treating the nine-box grid as an objective instrument instead of a structured judgement with known biases
- Describing talent programmes without evaluating whether they fit the organisation's context and chosen definition
- Handling attraction, development and retention as separate essays instead of one connected pipeline
- Ignoring the risks of the chosen approach, when naming the downside is exactly what evaluation means

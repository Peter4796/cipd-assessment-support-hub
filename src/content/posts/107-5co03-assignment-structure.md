5CO03 sets a structural problem no other Level 5 unit does: it asks for two kinds of writing in one submission. The discursive tasks want a neutral, evidenced, report-style voice. The reflective tasks want the first person, because they are about your own practice and what you learned from it. A submission that handles both registers cleanly reads as confident and professional. One that blurs them reads as confused, even when the underlying content is good.

This article deals with structure only: how to arrange a submission that contains both registers without jarring, how to shape the reflective and discursive sections themselves, and where the development planning element sits. For the unit as a whole, start with the 5CO03 complete guide, which is the hub of this series. One caveat before anything else: briefs vary by study centre and change over time, so treat everything here, especially the illustrative skeleton, as a way of thinking rather than a description of your actual brief. Your own current brief and your centre's guidance always come first.

## The two registers, and why structure solves the problem

The registers only jar when they collide inside a single answer: a paragraph of neutral analysis that suddenly lurches into personal anecdote, or a reflective account that retreats into textbook summary just as it should be getting personal. The fix is structural. Separate the registers at section level, so each section is written wholly in one voice, and the switch between them happens at a heading, where the reader expects a change.

Go through your brief and mark each task as discursive or reflective. Discursive tasks use verbs like explain, discuss, evaluate and assess, and they are answered in the third person with evidence and sources. Reflective tasks ask about your own practice, development or behaviour, and the first person is not just allowed there but expected. If a single task asks for both, split your answer into a discursive part and a reflective part under the same heading, in that order, so the personal material builds on the evidenced ground.

## Structuring a reflective section

A reflective section has four parts, and the proportions matter as much as the order.

1. Situation, briefly. What happened, who was involved and what your role was. Keep this to about a quarter of the section at most, because description on its own earns very little.
2. Analysis of your behaviour. Why you acted as you did, what assumptions or pressures shaped it, what worked and what did not. This is first person, specific and honest, and it should be the longest part.
3. Learning. What the episode revealed about how you work: a strength you can rely on, a habit that gets in your way, a gap you had not seen.
4. Action. What you will do differently, stated concretely enough that you could check later whether you did it.

The most common structural failure in reflective writing is inversion: three quarters story, then a single sentence of learning bolted on at the end. If your draft reads like a diary entry with a moral, rebalance it. Cut the description down to the minimum a stranger needs, and spend the recovered words on analysis and learning, because that is where the criteria in your brief are met.

> Integrity note: the reflective sections must be your own genuine experience. Invented or borrowed reflection defeats the purpose of the unit, and it tends to read as thin and generic to an experienced assessor.

## Structuring the discursive sections

The discursive tasks in 5CO03 cover ground like ethical behaviour, inclusion and wellbeing, and they follow the same evidenced-argument shape as any other Level 5 answer. Open by framing the question and defining key terms briefly. Present evidence from credible sources and from practice. Analyse what it means for people professionals and for your organisational context. Then land on a clear position or implication rather than trailing off.

The unit adds one twist: even discursive answers benefit from grounded examples of real professional situations, handled in a neutral voice. Describing what a people professional did and why it mattered is still discursive. Save the first person, and the examination of your own thinking, for the reflective sections, where they belong.

## Where the development plan sits

Most 5CO03 briefs include a professional development element, often a CPD record or development plan, and structurally it has one job: to grow out of the reflection. Place it directly after the reflective account, and build the link into the text. Let the reflection close by naming the gaps it exposed, and let the plan open by picking those gaps up as its priorities. Every development action should trace back to something the reflection actually found.

If your centre supplies a plan template or table, complete it as directed, keep it adjacent to the reflective account rather than exiled to the end of the document, and refer to it in your text so the marker sees the two as one piece of thinking. A plan the reflection never mentions, full of priorities the reflection never surfaced, reads as bolted on, and markers notice.

## Signposting between registers

Because the submission switches voice, the marker should always know which mode they are in, and the structure can tell them. Use headings that name the mode where the brief allows it, so a heading containing the word reflective or reflection warns the reader before the voice changes. Then confirm the register in the first sentence: a reflective section should reach the word I early, and a discursive section should open with neutral framing.

When you move from one register to the other, add a short transitional sentence so the shift feels deliberate. Something as simple as noting that the next section reflects on your own practice in relation to these themes is enough. The goal is that no reader ever pauses to wonder whether the voice change is intentional.

## An illustrative skeleton

Here is how the shape can come together for a generic 5CO03-style brief. This is an illustration only, not your brief: centres set different tasks with different weightings, so map the ideas onto the tasks you have actually been given.

1. Introduction setting scope and context. Around 5 per cent of the words.
2. Discursive section on professional and ethical behaviours, as an evidenced argument. Around 20 per cent.
3. Discursive section on championing inclusion and wellbeing, in the same shape. Around 20 per cent.
4. Discursive section on working with others and influencing, in the same shape. Around 15 per cent.
5. Reflective account in the first person, using the four-part shape with description held to a quarter. Around 25 per cent.
6. Development plan growing directly out of the gaps the reflection named. Around 10 per cent.
7. A brief conclusion and a complete, consistent Harvard reference list with the remaining words.

## A planning method for 5CO03

1. Label every task in your brief as discursive or reflective, and mark any that ask for both.
2. Gather reflective raw material before you plan: list real situations from your own practice that carried some tension, and pick the few that reveal most about how you work.
3. Plan the reflective sections first, around the four-part shape, noting the development gaps each reflection exposes.
4. Draft the development plan headings from those gaps, so the link between reflection and plan is built in rather than retrofitted.
5. Plan each discursive section as evidence, analysis and a landed position.
6. Budget words in proportion to the marks or criteria each task carries, protecting the analysis and learning parts of the reflection.
7. Draft, then reread checking register alone: every section should announce its mode in its heading or first sentence, with no drift in the middle.
8. Review the whole submission against the criteria in your brief, and check your centre's guidance on first person use.

## Common structural mistakes in 5CO03

- Reflection scattered in fragments through the discursive sections, so no single account ever goes deep enough to show real learning.
- A development plan bolted on at the end with no visible link to the reflection that should have produced it.
- Register drift: discursive answers that slide into personal anecdote, or reflective accounts that retreat into impersonal theory.
- Reflective sections that are three quarters description, with the learning compressed into a final sentence.
- Spending the reflective word count explaining a model like Gibbs or Kolb instead of quietly using it on an experience.
- Headings that give no clue which register follows, leaving the marker to work out the voice mid-paragraph.

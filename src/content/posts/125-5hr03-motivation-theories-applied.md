Learners preparing 5HR03, Reward for performance and contribution, usually know the motivation theories well enough. The trouble starts when the assignment asks them to use that knowledge on a scenario, and the recitation instinct takes over: paragraphs of textbook summary, followed by recommendations the theory never touches. Knowing a theory and applying one are different skills, and the marks sit almost entirely with the second.

If the theories themselves are hazy, start with Reward Principles in 5HR03, Explained, which introduces Herzberg's two-factor theory, Adams' equity theory and Vroom's expectancy theory and shows what each says about reward. This article assumes that grounding and deals with the harder skill: putting the theories to work inside answers, so they earn marks instead of filling space.

## Match the theory to the task

The fastest improvement available is simply choosing the right theory for the job. Each of the three main theories answers a different kind of question, which means the task type tells you which one to reach for.

Questions about fairness, comparison and perceived inequity point to Adams. If the scenario involves people comparing their deal with colleagues, new hires or the market, or a task asks why a technically adequate package is causing resentment, equity thinking is the tool.

Questions about incentive and bonus design point to Vroom. If the task asks you to design, assess or fix a scheme that links pay to results, expectancy thinking gives you the test to run against it.

Questions about why pay rises fail to motivate, or why morale stays flat despite decent pay, point to Herzberg. The hygiene and motivator distinction was built for exactly that puzzle.

Keep the mapping visible while you plan: comparison problems go to Adams, scheme design problems go to Vroom, and money-is-not-working problems go to Herzberg. It is a simplification, and some scenarios involve more than one, but as a first sorting rule it will place your theory correctly far more often than picking the one you remember best.

## The applied paragraph pattern

An applied theory paragraph makes four moves in order. First, a claim about the scenario, stated in plain terms. Second, the theory, named in a single clause rather than a paragraph of exposition. Third, the theory's prediction for this specific case, spelled out: what it says will happen here, to these people, under this arrangement. Fourth, the judgement you draw from that prediction.

The order matters because it keeps the scenario in charge. Most learners invert it, opening with the theory and hoping the scenario turns up later, which produces the textbook-summary feel markers penalise. The three worked paragraphs below show the pattern in action, each in a different invented organisation.

### Herzberg, applied to a pay rise that changed nothing

Calderline Freight is a fictional haulage firm that has raised driver pay twice in eighteen months, yet engagement and turnover have barely moved. This is the pattern Herzberg's two-factor theory would predict: pay behaves largely as a hygiene factor, so the rises will have eased active dissatisfaction with the deal but cannot, by themselves, generate positive motivation, which the theory locates in recognition, growth and the work itself. The prediction for Calderline is that further rises will buy diminishing quiet rather than commitment. The sensible response is to hold pay at a defensible level and direct the next round of spending at recognition and progression for drivers, which is where the theory says the motivational return actually sits.

### Adams, applied to a new-hire pay gap

Hartwell Claims is an invented insurance services firm that has been hiring claims handlers at salaries above those of its experienced staff to cope with a tight market. Adams' equity theory predicts trouble the pay budget alone would not reveal: existing handlers will compare their inputs and outcomes with the newcomers', judge the exchange unfair, and restore balance themselves through reduced effort, disengagement or exit. The prediction is specific, because the people most likely to leave are the experienced staff the firm can least afford to lose, having both the strongest sense of inequity and the best outside options. The judgement follows: Hartwell should close the internal gap or publish a credible route for existing staff to reach the new rates, because the hidden cost of perceived unfairness will outrun the visible cost of correction.

### Vroom, applied to a bonus nobody chases

Fenwick Homeware is a made-up retail chain whose store staff show no interest in an annual bonus tied to company-wide profit. Vroom's expectancy theory explains why in one pass: for a sales assistant, the link between individual effort and group profit is invisible, so the belief that effort leads to the reward fails at the first step and the scheme cannot motivate regardless of its size. The prediction is that the bonus will function as an occasional pleasant surprise, not an incentive. The design response is to move the measured performance closer to the person, for example store-level or team-level measures staff can actually influence, and to pay out on a cycle short enough for the connection to be felt.

## Using two theories together

Two theories can strengthen one answer when they are given different jobs. A natural pairing in reward tasks is Vroom on whether a scheme is well designed and Adams on how the scheme will be perceived once it exists: the first tests the mechanics, the second tests the fairness of its effects. Because the two answer different questions, their conclusions can sit side by side without competing.

The risk is contradiction. If you use Herzberg to argue that money does not motivate and then recommend a bonus scheme, the marker will notice, and one of the two has to give. Either qualify the theory's claim for your context or change the recommendation. Consistency between your theory and your advice can be checked at a glance, and it is one of the quickest ways markers separate assembled answers from reasoned ones.

## When not to use theory

Not every task wants theory. Descriptive and explanatory tasks, such as setting out the components of total reward or explaining how a pay structure works, are asking for accurate, organised knowledge of practice. Forcing a theory into them adds no marks and costs words you need elsewhere. Read the command verb: explain the practice means exactly that, and theory earns its place only where a task asks about motivation, behaviour or the likely effects of a reward choice. Your own current brief and your centre's guidance are the final word on what each task is asking, so check them before you plan.

## Critique at Level 5: one clause is enough

Level 7 asks for sustained critical engagement with theory; Level 5 does not. What Level 5 rewards is a signal that you know a theory is a model rather than a law, and a single clause carries that signal. Acknowledging in passing that Herzberg's methodology has been contested, or that expectancy theory assumes more deliberate calculation than most people consciously perform, is enough. Say it once, briefly, and return to the scenario. A page of critique in a Level 5 answer is not depth but displacement, and if you want to build the fuller skill for later study, How to Evaluate HR Theories Critically in CIPD Assignments covers it properly.

## Planning theory across a whole assignment

1. Go through the brief and mark every task that asks about motivation, behaviour or the likely effect of a reward choice.
2. Map each marked task to one theory using the sorting rule: comparison to Adams, scheme design to Vroom, money-not-working to Herzberg.
3. Cap the total, because two or three theories across the whole assignment, each used once and used deeply, is the right weight at this level.
4. Draft each theory paragraph with the four moves: scenario claim, theory in one clause, prediction for this case, judgement.
5. Check every prediction against your final recommendations, and fix any point where the theory and the advice pull in different directions.
6. Add one clause of limitation per theory, and no more.
7. Cut any paragraph that explains a theory without applying it, however well written it is.

> Tutor tip: the marker already knows what the theories say. Every sentence you spend re-explaining one is a sentence not spent earning application marks, so let the scenario lead and let the theory serve it.

## Common pitfalls

- Re-teaching the theory to the marker in paragraphs of pure exposition
- Cramming three theories into one paragraph, where none has room to predict anything
- Recommending an action your own theory paragraph argued against
- Misattributing ideas between theories, such as crediting Herzberg with effort-to-reward calculations or Adams with hygiene factors
- Treating critique as an essay-length requirement instead of a passing clause
- Using theory on descriptive tasks that asked for an explanation of practice
